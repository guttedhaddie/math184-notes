\section{Ancient Egypt}

\subsection*{Summary}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item Recorded civilization in Nile valley dates from c.\,3150\BC.
  
  \item Conquered 322\BC{} by Alexander the Great (Macedonia/Greece) who left his general Ptolemy in charge. Cleopatra's death in 30\BC{} marked the end of the Ptolemaic dynasty and Egypt became a Roman province. 
  
  \item Discovery of the Rosetta Stone (\AD 1799) containing Greek, hieroglyphic and demotic (post hieratic, cursive) Egyptian script, allowed translation of ancient Egyptian writing.
  
  \item Primary mathematical sources: Rhind/Ahmes (A'h-mose) papyrus c.\,1650\BC{} and the Moscow papyrus c.\,1700\BC.\footnote{Rhind was a Scottish egyptologist. Ahmes was the name of the scribe who wrote/copied the papyrus. The Moscow papyrus is named because it was sold to the Moscow Museum of Fine Art; its author is unknown.} The Rhind papyrus contains around a hundred worked problem and two tables: unit fraction representations of $\frac 2n$ for integers $n<100$, and expressions for $\frac 3{10},\frac 4{10},\ldots,\frac 9{10}$ in terms of unit fractions.\par
  The Moscow papyrus is shorter and more focused on geometric problems.
  \begin{figure}[h]
	  \begin{center}
	  	%\includegraphics[scale=0.4]{rhindwhole.jpg}\\
	  	\includegraphics[scale=0.8,alt={the Rhind mathematical papyrus}]{Rhind_Mathematical_Papyrus.jpg}\\
	  	Part of the Rhind Papyrus
	  \end{center}
  \end{figure}
  
  \item Few other primary sources. Egyptians wrote on papyrus, a plant-based early form of paper, which naturally decomposes. Other Egyptian mathematics was likely absorbed uncredited by the Greeks.
  \item Egyptian mathematics was practical \& non-abstract. Worked problems on sums, linear equations, construction and land-measurement (tax-collection). No clear distinction between exact and approximate solutions.
\end{itemize}



\subsection*{Notation}

Ancient Egypt had two distinct systems of enumeration. These changed over time, so we give only a loose overview.

\begin{description}
	\item[Hieroglyphic enumeration] Dating from at least 5000\BC, used pictorial symbols, one for each power of 10. Grouped and stacked together to make larger numbers, smaller digits were often on the left: e.g.
	\[
		2342=\egyptify{0}{0}{0}{2}{3}{4}{2}
	\]
	Hieroglyphs were cumbersome to use for calculations since large numbers needed many symbols, but were more appropriate for \href{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kom_Ombo_Temple_Calendar_2.JPG}{carving into stone}.
	
	\item[Hieratic enumeration]	A cursive system dating from c.{} 2000\BC, as seen on the Rhind papyrus. Symbols for 1--9, 10--90, 100--900 etc., were mapped onto the hieratic alphabet, an approach copied later by the Greeks with their own alphabet.\par
	As suggested in the picture, 23 would be written (approximately!) $|||\cl{\wedge}$.\smallbreak
\end{description}

Ancient Eygpt had no numeral or symbol for zero, though the hieroglyph \emph{nfr} (beautiful/perfect) was used to indicate balanced books in accounting or the base level of a building.



\subsection*{Egyptian Fractions}

We are used to both decimal and fractional notation: e.g. $0.95=\frac{19}{20}$. Ancient Egyptians would instead have viewed this quantity as a sum of \emph{reciprocals of integers}:
\[
	\frac 12+\frac 14+\frac 15
\]
Quantities represented in this fashion are still referred to as \emph{Egyptian fractions}; they are still actively researched.\par
In hieratic notation, a dot was placed above a symbol to denote the reciprocal: e.g., $\dot{\wedge}$ is $\frac 1{10}$. In hieroglyphs, an oval was used instead. We do this with a bar: e.g. $\cl 2=\frac 12$.\par

There are several approaches to finding Egyptian fraction representations, and it can be proved that any positive rational number may be written in such a form. If the denominator is odd, then a simple place to start is with
\[
	\frac 2{mn}=\frac 1{mr}+\frac 1{nr}\quad\text{where}\quad r=\frac{m+n}2
\]
Many, but not all, lines in the Rhind table follow this formula. Note also that the formula often permits multiple representations.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The line for $\frac 25$ has $m=1$, $n=5$ and $r=3$; this is unique up to reordering.
  \item $\frac 29$ may be represented
  \[
  	\frac 29=
  	\begin{cases}
  		\frac 19+\frac 19&(m,n,r)=(3,3,3)\\
  		\frac 15+\frac 1{45}&(m,n,r)=(1,9,5) 	
  	\end{cases}
  \]
  The first of these is essentially useless and the second isn't the expression from the table.
  \item In the table, $\frac 2{13}$ is written as a sum of three reciprocals instead of two.
\end{itemize}

The table reduced the need to divide and sped up the computation of harder fractions: for instance,\phantomsection\label{ex:rhinddivide}
\[
	\frac 5{13} =2\cdot\frac 2{13}+\frac 1{13} =2\left(\frac 18+\frac 1{52}+\frac 1{104}\right)+\frac 1{13} =\frac 14+\frac 1{13}+\frac 1{26}+\frac 1{52}
\]



\subsection*{Calculations \& Example Problems}

Egyptian notation is not computationally efficient. Hieroglyphs took a long time to write, and cursive notation required many symbols. Regardless, we have some evidence of practical calculation methods and many worked problems form the papyri.


\subsubsection*{Addition/Subtraction}

In practice this would likely have been done with carrying and borrowing, though details are scant. A special symbol was used to denote both addition and subtraction, with its meaning changing depending on the direction the text was read.\smallbreak
Carrying and borrowing would have been very simple with hieroglyphs: write out numbers one above another and count up the symbols, replacing ten of one symbol by the next. For subtraction, one might need to convert a larger symbol into ten of a smaller one. 


\subsubsection*{Multiplication}

This relied on a base-two algorithm. To compute the product $ab$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write 1, $b$.
  \item Double each term repeatedly until the first term is about to exceed $a$.
  \item Determine powers of 2 that sum to $a$.
  \item Sum the corresponding multiples of $b$.
\end{enumerate}
For example, to compute $13\cdot 15$ we construct a table where the checked rows are summed.
\[
	\begin{array}{r@{\qquad}r@{\qquad}c}
		1&15&\checkmark\\
		2&30&\\
		4&60&\checkmark\\
		8&120&\checkmark
	\end{array}
	\quad\implies 13\cdot 15=(1+4+8)\cdot 15=15+60+120=195
\]
Note how $13=1+4+8=2^0+2^2+2^3$ is essentially the binary representation. We stopped at the fourth row since another doubling would have resulted in the first term (16) exceeding 13. We could instead have reversed the roles of the factors:
\[
	\begin{array}{r@{\qquad}r@{\qquad}c}
		1&13&\checkmark\\
		2&26&\checkmark\\
		4&52&\checkmark\\
		8&104&\checkmark
	\end{array}
	\quad\implies 15\cdot 13=(1+2+4+8)\cdot 13=13+26+52+104=195
\]
All you need for this method is addition and the ability to multiply by 2!


\subsubsection*{Division}

This also relies on doubling and halving, though the answer is non-unique. To compute $\frac ab$, we think about the problem $a=bx$ and apply a variant of the multiplication algorithm to find multiples of $b$ summing to $a$. Here are two examples.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item To compute $260\div 13$, we repeatedly double 13 until terms in the right column sum to 260.
	\[
		\begin{array}{r@{\qquad}r@{\qquad}c}
			1&13\\
			2&26\\
			4&52&\checkmark\\
			8&104\\
			16&208&\checkmark
		\end{array}
	\]
	Since $260=208+52$ we conclude that $260\div 13=16+4=20$.

	%\item $\frac{271}{13}$: We would continue the above table, noting that

	\item To find $5\div 13$, start dividing by 2 with the intent of making terms in the right column sum to 5.
	\[
		\begin{array}{r@{\qquad}r@{\qquad}c}
1&13\\
			\cl 2&6\ \cl 2\\
			\cl 4&3\ \cl 4&\checkmark\\
			\cl 8&1\ \cl 2\ \cl 8&\checkmark
		\end{array}
	\]
	Since $\big(3\ \cl 4\big)+\big(1\ \cl 2\ \cl 8\big) =4\ \cl 2\ \cl 4\ \cl 8$ is $\cl 8$ ($\frac 18$) short of what we want, we continue the table differently. Divide by 13 and continue halving until we obtain the desired $\cl 8$ in the right column.
	\[
		\begin{array}{r@{\qquad}r@{\qquad}c}
			\cl{13}&1\\
			\cl{26}&\cl 2\\
			\cl{52}&\cl 4\\
			\cl{104}&\cl 8&\checkmark
		\end{array}
		\quad\implies 5\div 13=\cl 4\ \cl 8\ \cl{104} =\frac 14+\frac 18+\frac 1{104}
	\]
	In practice, a scribe likely used a table such as that in the Rhind papyrus to obtain the same answer (see page \pageref{ex:rhinddivide}). Note also that the method is non-unique: we could instead have checked the third, fifth, sixth and seventh rows of the above table to obtain
	\[
		5=(3\ \cl 4)+1+\cl 2+\cl 4\implies 5\div 13=\cl 4\ \cl{13}\ \cl{26}\ \cl{52}
	\]
\end{enumerate}



\paragraph{Practical application: Loaf-splitting}

A typical Egyptian problem might involve determining how to split 5 loaves among 13 people. The previous calculation says that we could give each person $\frac 14+\frac 18+\frac 1{104}$ of a loaf. This might seem complicated but it has some advantages over a typical modern approach (cut each loaf into 13 equal pieces and give each person five):
\begin{itemize}
  \item The Egyptian approach repeatedly cuts pieces of bread in half. This is easy to do with reasonable accuracy, at least for the $\frac 14$ and $\frac 18$ pieces, and in a way that most people would consider fair. Cutting a loaf into 13\th{} parts is difficult! The remaining 104\th{} parts of a loaf would probably be ignored as crumbs.
  \item Even with full accuracy, the Egyptian approach requires 34 cuts, reduced to 22 cuts if the 104\th{} parts are ignored. The modern approach requires 60 cuts.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Linear equations}

Solutions were based on the method of \emph{false position}: guess an approximate solution and modifies it until it works. Here is problem 24 of the Rhind papyrus.
\begin{quote}
	A heap plus a seventh of a heap is nineteen. What is the heap?
\end{quote}
In modern algebra, we wish to solve $x+\frac 17x=19$ where $x$ is the 'heap.' Here is the Egyptian approach.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.69\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\begin{enumerate}
  	\item Guess intelligently: $x=14$ is easy to divide by 7 and produces $x+\frac 17 x=16$.
  	\item Correct our guess: We want 19, so we multiply our guess (14) by $\frac{19}{16}=1\ \cl 8\ \cl{16}$ to obtain the correct answer
  	\[
  		x=14\big(1\ \cl 8\ \cl{16}\big) =2\ \cl 4\ \cl 8 +4\ \cl 2\ \cl 4+ 9\ \cl 2 =16\ \cl 2\ \cl 8
  	\]
	\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
	\hfill 
	\[
		\begin{array}{r@{\qquad}r@{\qquad}c}
			1&1\ \cl 8\ \cl{16}\\
			2&2\ \cl 4\ \cl 8&\checkmark\\
			4&4\ \cl 2\ \cl 4&\checkmark\\
			8&9\ \cl 2&\checkmark
		\end{array}
	\]
\end{minipage}\smallbreak
Compare this to the `modern' method:
\[
	x+\frac 17x=19\implies \frac 87x=19\implies x=\frac{7\cdot 19}8 =\frac{133}8 =\frac{128+5}8= 16\frac 58
\]
Are the any benefits to the Egyptian approach?


\subsubsection*{Geometry}

Problem 48 of the Rhind papyrus uses an octagon to approximate the area of a circle.\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.67\linewidth}\vspace{-6pt}
	A square of side 9 is drawn, where each side is split into thirds and the four corner squares are cut in half. The area of the octagon is then
	\[
		81-18=63
	\]
	Since the area of the circle is $\frac{81\pi}4$, this amounts to the approximation $\pi\approx\frac{28}9=3.11111\ldots$\medbreak
	
	Problem 50 compares the same circle to a square of side 8. This time $\frac{81\pi}4\approx 64$ corresponds to $\pi\approx \frac{256}{81}=3.16049\ldots$
\end{minipage}
\hfill
